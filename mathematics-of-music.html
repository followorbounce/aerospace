THE MATHEMATICS OF MUSIC, FROM A VIBRATING STRING TO A RADIO SIGNAL

CH.01 / 14 — WHAT IS SOUND, PHYSICALLY

Music starts as nothing more than air bumping into itself

Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave: a vibrating object — a string, a speaker cone, a vocal fold — pushes on the air next to it, compressing it, and that compressed air pushes the air next to it, and so on outward in every direction. No single air molecule travels far from where it started; what actually moves outward is the disturbance itself, the same way a wave crossing a stadium crowd is people momentarily standing and sitting, not people running around the building.

A microphone, an eardrum, and an oscilloscope all do the same job: they sit still and record how the local air pressure rises and falls over time as that disturbance passes through. Every sound this guide discusses — a plucked guitar string, a human voice, a spacecraft's radio beacon — reduces to that one measurement: pressure, or its electrical stand-in, voltage, changing shape over time. Chapter 2 gives that changing shape a precise vocabulary.

THE WAVE EQUATION — TRUE FOR SOUND, LIGHT, AND RADIO ALIKE
v = f · λ
v = wave speed (≈343 m/s for sound in air at 20°C), f = frequency, λ = wavelength. Chapter 11 runs this exact same equation at radio frequencies, where v becomes the speed of light.

CH.02 / 14 — THE FOUR NUMBERS THAT DESCRIBE ANY WAVE

Frequency, amplitude, wavelength, and phase — and you've described any sound wave that exists

Frequency is how many pressure cycles pass a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz) — it's the physical quantity the ear perceives as pitch. Amplitude is how large the pressure swing is, perceived as loudness. Wavelength is the physical distance one full cycle occupies in space, λ = v/f — a low note's wavelength in air can be several metres long, while a high note's is a few centimetres.

Phase is where a wave sits within its own cycle at a given instant, usually measured in degrees (0–360°) or radians. It seems like the least important of the four right up until Chapter 7, where adding waves of the same frequency but different phase is exactly how instruments build their characteristic sound — and Chapter 11, where it's the third thing, besides amplitude and frequency, that a radio signal can be modulated to carry information.

CH.03 / 14 — WHY A NOTE IS JUST A FREQUENCY WITH A NAME

The world agreed, in writing, that this exact pitch is called “A”

Pitch is what the ear perceives when it hears a frequency; music assigns twelve letter-named pitch classes (A, A#, B, C, C#…) that repeat every octave, so a note's full identity needs both a letter and an octave number. That system is scientific pitch notation: C4 is “middle C,” A4 is the A above it, and A4 is fixed, by international agreement, at exactly 440 Hz — the reference every other note on this page is computed from.

440 Hz wasn't always the standard — concert pitch drifted anywhere from about 415 Hz to over 450 Hz across different eras and countries before it was formally standardized at 440 Hz by ISO in 1975 (Reference 1), and some orchestras today still tune slightly sharp of it, around 442–443 Hz. The letter names themselves also aren't universal: Russian musical training generally uses solfège syllables — До, Ре, Ми, Фа, Соль, Ля, Си — for the same seven pitch classes as C, D, E, F, G, A, B.

CH.04 / 14 — DOUBLING THE FREQUENCY, DOUBLING THE OCTAVE

Every octave up is the same note, played by a wave twice as fast

An octave is defined as a 2:1 frequency ratio — nothing more exotic than that. Starting from A4 = 440 Hz, one octave up is 880 Hz, another octave up is 1760 Hz; going down, 440 Hz becomes 220 Hz, then 110 Hz, then 55 Hz. Every tuning system in history, however much it disagrees about the notes in between (Chapters 9–10 cover that disagreement), has always treated the octave as the one interval nobody disputes.

Why does doubled frequency sound like “the same note, higher” rather than a different note entirely? Because a 2:1 ratio is the tightest possible lock two different frequencies can have: every single peak of the higher wave lines up with a peak of the lower wave, cycle after cycle, with no exceptions. Chapter 9 shows that as the ratio gets less perfectly aligned — 3:2, 4:3, 5:4 — the ear hears two increasingly distinct notes instead of one doubled note.

CH.05 / 14 — WHY A SHORTER STRING SINGS HIGHER

A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in a few exact shapes

Pluck a string clamped at both ends and it reflects waves back and forth off each fixed point. Only wavelengths that fit exactly between the two ends — with a stationary “node” at each end — survive; everything else cancels itself out through interference within a few reflections. The simplest surviving shape, the fundamental, is a single hump whose wavelength is exactly twice the string's length: λ₁ = 2L.

Combine that with Chapter 1's v = f·λ and the string's fundamental frequency falls straight out: f₁ = v/2L. Everything a guitarist or pianist does to change pitch is manipulating one of these two variables — fretting a string shortens L and raises pitch; tightening a tuning peg raises the tension T and therefore the wave speed v, which also raises pitch. Try both below.

FUNDAMENTAL FREQUENCY OF A STRING
f₁ = v / (2L)
L = speaking length between the two fixed ends; v = wave speed along the string itself, not the speed of sound in air.
WAVE SPEED ALONG THE STRING
v = √(T / μ)
T = tension, μ = mass per unit length. A thicker or wound string raises μ, lowering v and pitch without needing extra length — why a piano's bass strings are wound rather than simply long.

CH.06 / 14 — ONE STRING, MANY FREQUENCIES AT ONCE

A plucked string never vibrates in just one shape

Chapter 5 covered only the simplest standing-wave shape. A real plucked, bowed, or struck string vibrates in its fundamental and, simultaneously, every integer multiple of it — a second mode with two humps and an extra node, a third with three, and on upward — all present together, at different strengths, added on top of each other. Each of these modes is called a harmonic, and their frequencies are exact integer multiples of the fundamental: fₙ = n · f₁.

This series quietly contains Chapter 9's entire table of consonant intervals: the 2nd harmonic sits an octave above the fundamental (2:1), the interval between the 2nd and 3rd harmonics is a perfect fifth (3:2), between the 3rd and 4th a perfect fourth (4:3), and between the 4th and 5th a major third (5:4). The “simple ratios” Chapter 9 treats as a historical discovery are, physically, just the gaps between adjacent rungs of this one ladder.

THE HARMONIC SERIES
fₙ = n · f₁   (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5…)
A valveless bugle or hunting horn can only play notes from this one series — it has no way to change L or v, so n is the only thing a player controls.

CH.07 / 14 — WHY A FLUTE AND A VIOLIN PLAYING THE SAME NOTE SOUND NOTHING ALIKE

Two instruments can share every frequency and still sound completely different

Pitch depends only on the fundamental, f₁ — but Chapter 6 showed every real note also carries a whole stack of harmonics on top of it, and it's the relative loudness of each harmonic, its “recipe,” that gives an instrument its timbre. Two notes can share an identical f₁ and be instantly distinguishable, because a flute's recipe is almost pure fundamental while a violin's is loud all the way up its harmonic series.

This is Fourier's insight, generalized beyond strings: any periodic wave whatsoever, no matter how jagged it looks, can be built by adding up pure sine waves at the right frequencies, amplitudes, and phases (Chapter 2's fourth number, finally put to work). Fourier analysis is the math that finds those amplitudes from a recorded sound; a spectrogram plots them continuously over time — and Chapter 11's radio engineers read a signal's spectrum with exactly the same tool.

FOURIER SYNTHESIS, INFORMALLY
s(t) = Σₙ Aₙ · sin(2π n f₁ t + φₙ)

CH.08 / 14 — THE FAMILIAR SHAPES A SPECTRUM CAN BUILD

Sine, square, sawtooth, triangle, and noise are just five different recipes

Chapter 7's “recipe” idea has a few extremely common, named results worth knowing by sight and by ear. A sine wave — or a cosine, the exact same shape, just starting a quarter-cycle (90°) earlier — is the purest possible tone: one frequency, one amplitude, nothing else in its spectrum. A plain amplitude spectrum can't show phase at all, which is why a sine and a cosine look identical in the spectrum view below despite looking different on the oscilloscope — Chapter 2 already called phase the one number that only matters once waves are combined.

A square wave switches instantly between two levels and contains only odd harmonics (f, 3f, 5f…), each falling off as 1/n — the buzzy, hollow core of classic synthesizer basses (Chapter 2 already showed a real digital signal isn't perfectly square either). A sawtooth, or ramp, wave rises steadily and drops instantly, containing every harmonic, odd and even alike, also falling as 1/n — the brightest, buzziest classic analog-synth lead, and literally the shape of an oscilloscope's own timebase sweep. A triangle wave has only odd harmonics too, like a square wave, but they fall off much faster, as 1/n², sounding noticeably softer and closer to a pure tone.

Noise is the opposite extreme: instead of a few clean harmonics, it has energy at every frequency at once, with no periodicity at all — there's no f₁ to even measure. White noise carries equal energy at every frequency (a flat spectrum, literally every pitch at once, equally loud) and sounds like a hiss, or an untuned radio between stations. Pink noise tilts that spectrum down, losing power as 1/f so that each octave carries equal energy instead of each frequency — closer to how the ear actually weighs loudness across its range — and sounds smoother, like steady rainfall.

Brown (or “red”) noise tilts further still, losing power as 1/f² and shedding high frequencies fastest of all — a deep rumble, like a distant waterfall. Every shape on this page reappears verbatim in electronics: a sawtooth is a radar chirp's building block, a square wave is the idealized digital signal CASCADE's own second chapter complicates with real rise times, and noise is the fundamental floor every radio receiver in Chapter 11 has to work above. CH.09 / 14 — CONSONANCE IS A SIMPLE FRACTION

The most pleasant-sounding intervals are also the simplest fractions

An interval is just the ratio between two frequencies. Chapter 6 already derived where the simplest ones come from: the octave (2:1), perfect fifth (3:2), perfect fourth (4:3), and major third (5:4) are literally the gaps between neighboring harmonics. Tradition credits the Pythagorean school in ancient Greece with first formalizing this — using a monochord, a single string with a movable bridge, to show that simple string-length ratios produce the intervals musicians already recognized by ear.

(The popular story of Pythagoras discovering this by listening to blacksmiths' hammering is a much later legend, and not physically sound — differently-massed hammers don't ring at clean harmonic ratios; the monochord tradition is the well-attested part.) Interval size is usually measured in cents, a logarithmic unit with exactly 1200 per octave and 100 per equal-tempered semitone, defined as cents = 1200 · log₂(f₂/f₁) — Chapter 10 needs that unit to measure just how far equal temperament drifts from these pure ratios.

CH.10 / 14 — THE COMPROMISE THAT LET MUSIC MODULATE FREELY

Every semitone is now the same ratio — and no pure interval survives perfectly

Try to build a full keyboard from Chapter 9's pure ratios and it breaks: stack twelve pure perfect fifths (3:2) and you should land back on the note you started from, seven octaves higher — but (3/2)¹² ÷ 2⁷ ≈ 1.0136, a mismatch of about 23.5 cents known as the Pythagorean comma. An instrument tuned this way plays in tune in some keys and audibly sour, a “wolf interval,” in others.

Twelve-tone equal temperament's fix is to stop chasing pure ratios altogether: divide the octave into 12 mathematically identical steps, so every key is equally, slightly imperfect rather than some keys being perfect and others unusable. A tempered fifth (700¢) ends up only ≈2¢ flat of a pure one (702¢) — nearly inaudible — but a tempered major third (400¢) sits ≈13.7¢ sharp of a pure one (386.3¢), which is exactly why fifths still sound clean on a modern piano while thirds sound faintly buzzy to a trained ear.

12-TONE EQUAL TEMPERAMENT
f = f₀ · 2^(n/12)
f₀ = reference pitch (A4 = 440 Hz throughout this page), n = signed number of semitones away. The 12 semitones of one octave form the chromatic scale: C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B.

CH.11 / 14 — A CARRIER WAVE IS JUST A VERY FAST, INAUDIBLE NOTE

Radio and music are the same physics, several octaves apart

Every idea since Chapter 1 — v = f·λ, amplitude, phase, superposition — applies unchanged to electromagnetic waves; only what's oscillating (an electric and magnetic field, not air pressure) and the frequencies involved change. A radio “carrier wave” is a pure sine wave exactly like Chapter 2's oscilloscope trace, just oscillating millions of times faster than anything the ear can hear, at the speed of light rather than the speed of sound.

Modulation borrows music's own vocabulary: amplitude modulation (AM) varies the carrier's amplitude in step with an audio signal, exactly like Chapter 2's amplitude slider; frequency modulation (FM) varies the carrier's frequency in step with the signal — mechanically identical to a singer's vibrato, just happening millions of times a second instead of five or six. The range of frequencies a signal occupies is its bandwidth — radio engineering's name for exactly the “how many harmonics does this contain” question Chapter 7 asked about a musical note.

CH.12 / 14 — THE UNIVERSE'S OWN RADIO STATIONS

Some of the most precise clocks and loudest transmitters that exist were never built by anyone

NASA's Deep Space Network relays every spacecraft's telemetry as a modulated radio carrier (Chapter 11) across hundreds of millions of kilometres, mainly on the X-band frequencies near 8.4 GHz marked on the axis above (Reference 5). Some missions have also carried the idea back down into the audible range on purpose: Mars Pathfinder's 1997 landing used a set of short, distinct audio tones, composed specifically so mission controllers could follow the landing sequence by ear as each one played.

The universe also broadcasts on its own. Jupiter's magnetic field, swept past its volcanic moon Io, accelerates electrons that generate powerful natural “decametric” radio bursts — early radio astronomers fed receivers like this straight to a loudspeaker and genuinely listened (Reference 7). Millisecond pulsars — neutron stars spinning hundreds of times a second, sweeping a radio beam past Earth on every rotation — are, per NASA, nature's most precise natural clocks, with timing stability that rivals engineered atomic clocks (Reference 4).

Deep Space Network — three dishes, always one facing the sky

+
Three antenna complexes — Goldstone (California), Madrid (Spain), and Canberra (Australia) — sit roughly 120° apart in longitude, so as Earth rotates a spacecraft out of view of one, another is already rising to take over (Reference 5). Chapter 11's X-band marker (~8.4 GHz) is the DSN's main deep-space downlink band today; newer missions are moving to Ka-band (~32 GHz) for more bandwidth.

The Voyager Golden Record — 90 minutes of music, aimed at 40,000 years from now

+
Each Voyager spacecraft carries a gold-plated phonograph record encoding greetings in 55 languages, sounds of Earth, 115 images, and an eclectic 90-minute selection of music spanning Eastern and Western traditions (Reference 3) — an attempt to encode Chapter 7's timbre and Chapter 9's intervals for a listener who may never exist. NASA notes it will be roughly 40,000 years before either spacecraft makes a close approach to another planetary system.
CH.13 / 14 — THE SAME RATIOS, WRITTEN IN ORBITS

Pythagoras's ratios turn out to describe orbits as readily as strings

Johannes Kepler took the Pythagorean tradition from Chapter 9 further than anyone: his 1619 Harmonices Mundi (“Harmony of the World”) searched for, and believed he had found, musical-interval-like ratios in the relative orbital speeds of the planets. It was a mathematically serious but explicitly mystical framework — and it's worth being precise that this was never a literal claim about audible sound: space is a vacuum, and Chapter 1's pressure waves have no medium to travel through between planets.

What's real, and thoroughly documented, is orbital resonance: Jupiter's moons Io, Europa, and Ganymede lock into almost exactly a 1:2:4 ratio of orbital periods — for every single orbit Ganymede completes, Europa completes two and Io completes four, enforced continuously by gravity rather than by a vibrating string. It's the identical integer-ratio logic as Chapter 6's harmonic series and Chapter 9's intervals, just running on orbital mechanics instead of acoustics; the TRAPPIST-1 system, discovered decades later, shows a comparable chain of near-resonant planets. The kinship is genuinely mathematical — periodic systems locking into small integer ratios — not a claim that either system makes a sound.

CH.14 / 14 — ONE EQUATION, READ IN EVERY DIRECTION

Music was never a separate subject from physics — it was the first one anyone measured

Read this guide forward: a vibration (Ch.1) becomes a pressure wave with a definite frequency (Ch.2); frequency gets a name as a note (Ch.3) that repeats every doubling (Ch.4); a string's fixed length forces that frequency into existence (Ch.5) as a whole stack of harmonics (Ch.6) whose relative strengths are a Fourier “recipe” called timbre (Ch.7) — a recipe that also produces a handful of named, recognizable shapes, and, run in reverse, describes noise, which has no single frequency at all (Ch.8). The ratios between harmonics are the simple fractions that define consonance (Ch.9), which equal temperament trades for one modulation-friendly compromise (Ch.10).

Read it wider: the exact same wave equation, run at frequencies millions of times higher, is a radio carrier (Ch.11) linking Earth to spacecraft across the Solar System (Ch.12) — and, Kepler's mysticism aside, shows up again as literal integer-ratio locks between orbiting moons (Ch.13). There is no point on this map where “music stops and physics starts.” A struck piano string and a modulated microwave downlink are the same equation, evaluated at wildly different frequencies, for wildly different audiences.

“The heavenly motions are nothing but a continuous song for several voices, to be perceived by the intellect, not by the ear.”

JOHANNES KEPLER, HARMONICES MUNDI, 1619
