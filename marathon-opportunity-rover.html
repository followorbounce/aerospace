CH.01 / 10 — A HOLE-IN-ONE LANDING

Bouncing straight into the one crater worth landing in

Opportunity landed in Meridiani Planum on January 25, 2004, at 05:05 UTC, three weeks after its twin Spirit touched down on the opposite side of Mars.[2] Cushioned by airbags, it bounced and rolled to a stop inside a small crater only about 22 metres across — a landing so improbable that mission scientists nicknamed it a "hole-in-one."[5]

That crater, later named Eagle, happened to expose bedrock right at the surface — features Opportunity would otherwise have had to drive for weeks to find, delivered instead by the very bounce that could have ended the mission on landing day.[5]

CH.02 / 10 — BLUEBERRIES IN EAGLE CRATER

Evidence of water, found in the first week

Almost immediately, Opportunity's cameras spotted small, spherical grey pebbles scattered across the crater floor, quickly nicknamed "blueberries." Later analysis identified them as concretions of hematite, an iron-oxide mineral that on Earth typically forms in the presence of liquid water — placing solid evidence for ancient water in Opportunity's hands before its mission was even a month old.[4]

CH.03 / 10 — ANATOMY OF OPPORTUNITY

An identical twin, built from the same blueprint

Opportunity shared Spirit's exact 185 kg design, six-wheeled rocker-bogie suspension, and full instrument suite.[4] Select a component.

CH.04 / 10 — ENDURANCE AND THE METEORITE

A find nobody was even looking for

After months studying Eagle Crater and its surroundings, Opportunity moved on to the larger Endurance Crater, spending the second half of 2004 studying its deeper, older rock layers. Along the way it happened upon an iron-nickel rock later named Heat Shield Rock — the first meteorite ever identified on the surface of another planet.[7]

CH.05 / 10 — TWO YEARS AT VICTORIA CRATER

Reading a cliff face like the pages of a book

Opportunity reached the much larger Victoria Crater in 2006 and spent roughly two years studying its scalloped rim and layered walls, which preserved a longer record of wind, dust, and possible water activity than any site studied so far — all while surviving a planet-wide dust storm in 2007 that briefly threatened both rovers.

CH.06 / 10 — THE LONG DRIVE TO ENDEAVOUR

Three years driving toward a crater twenty-two kilometres wide

In 2008, mission scientists set Opportunity on a years-long trek toward Endeavour Crater, roughly 22 km across — far larger and geologically older than anything explored before. The rover finally arrived in August 2011, a journey long enough that the team began calling Endeavour a genuine "second landing site."

CH.07 / 10 — CLAY MINERALS AND GENTLER WATER

Rewriting how habitable ancient Mars really was

At Endeavour, in an area later named Marathon Valley, Opportunity found clay minerals that form only in water with a much closer-to-neutral pH than the highly acidic conditions suggested by the earlier hematite "blueberries." Together, the two findings sketched a Martian past that shifted over time from milder, more Earth-like water chemistry toward harsher, more acidic conditions.

CH.08 / 10 — RUNNING A MARATHON ON MARS

42.195 kilometres, a rover speed of a few centimetres a second

In March 2015, Opportunity's total odometer reading passed 42.195 km — the exact length of an Olympic marathon — a milestone NASA celebrated explicitly, timed only days before the Boston Marathon, as proof of just how far a solar-powered robot could travel one careful drive at a time.

CH.09 / 10 — THE DUST STORM THAT ENDED IT

Silenced by weather, not by wear

On June 10, 2018 — Sol 5111 — a Mars-wide dust storm darkened Opportunity's skies so completely that its solar panels could no longer generate meaningful power, and the rover fell silent from Perseverance Valley, near Endeavour's rim.[3]

After the storm cleared weeks later, the mission team radiated over 1,000 commands toward the rover, hoping dust had simply settled off its panels rather than caused permanent damage.[3] None were answered. NASA declared the mission complete on February 13, 2019, after search and recovery efforts formally concluded the day before.[1][3]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

Fifty-seven times longer than its warranty

Designed for a 90-sol mission, Opportunity operated for over 5,000 sols — roughly fourteen years and forty-six days on Earth's calendar, covering more than 45 kilometres, farther than any other wheeled vehicle has ever driven off the surface of Earth.[4][9]
