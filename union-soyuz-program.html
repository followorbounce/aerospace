CH.01 / 10 — A DESIGN REFINED FOR SIXTY YEARS, NOT REPLACED

Nearly every crewed Soviet and Russian flight has used this shape

Soyuz has flown in some form continuously since 1967, making it the longest-serving crewed spacecraft design in the world.[1] Dozens of variants — 7K, T, TM, TMA, and today's MS — have upgraded its electronics, computers, and docking systems, but the three-module shape and the basic re-entry capsule have stayed recognisably the same.

CH.02 / 10 — THE FIRST DEATH OF THE SPACE AGE

A parachute failure on a spacecraft rushed to fly

Soyuz's first crewed flight, on April 23, 1967, ended in tragedy: cosmonaut Vladimir Komarov died when the descent module's main parachute failed to deploy properly during re-entry.[2] It was the first in-flight death in the history of spaceflight, and it forced a pause and redesign before Soyuz flew with a crew again.

CH.03 / 10 — THREE DEATHS, AND A RULE THAT STILL APPLIES

A cabin that lost its air, and cosmonauts who wore no suits to stop it

Returning from history's first space station, Salyut 1, the crew of Soyuz 11 died when a valve jolted open during separation and the descent module lost all its air; they were not wearing pressure suits, since the cramped three-seat cabin of the day had no room for them.[2] Every Soyuz crew since has worn a pressure suit for launch and landing, and the capsule was redesigned to carry only two so the suits would fit — a limit later variants engineered their way back out of.

CH.05 / 10 — A ROCKET WHOSE ONLY JOB IS TO OUTRUN DISASTER

Built to fire in the worst seconds of the mission

Perched above the payload shroud, the launch escape tower carries its own solid rocket motors, aimed to fire away from the booster the instant a launch failure is detected — on the pad or during the earliest, most dangerous minutes of ascent. It hauls the orbital and descent modules clear, then releases them to parachute down separately, with no input needed from the crew.[3]

CH.06 / 10 — A PAD FIRE AND AN ASCENT FAILURE, BOTH SURVIVED

Seconds before an explosion, twice, the crew simply left

In September 1983, a Soyuz booster caught fire on the launch pad; the escape tower fired about two seconds before the rocket exploded, pulling cosmonauts Vladimir Titov and Gennadi Strekalov away under a brutal but survivable 17g and landing them safely nearby.[3] In October 2018, Soyuz MS-10 suffered a booster separation failure during ascent; the escape system triggered again, and Aleksei Ovchinin and Nick Hague rode a ballistic descent back to a safe landing. Both times, the system did exactly the one job it exists for.

CH.07 / 10 — SALYUT, MIR, AND THE ISS

The same capsule shape has docked with every Soviet and Russian station

Soyuz has carried crews to every Salyut station, to Mir across its entire 15-year life, and to the International Space Station since the first permanent crew arrived in 2000.[1] Across more than half a century it has been upgraded again and again for automated docking, digital avionics, and new safety systems, but its role has never changed: a reliable taxi to and from a station in orbit.

CH.08 / 10 — NINE YEARS AS THE ONLY WAY TO THE ISS

After the shuttle retired, every astronaut flew Russian

When the Space Shuttle flew its last mission in 2011, NASA had no replacement ready. For nearly nine years, every astronaut bound for the ISS — American, European, Japanese, Canadian — flew there on a Soyuz launched from Baikonur, under contracts that eventually cost NASA over $80 million per seat.[1] That dependence ended only when SpaceX's Crew Dragon flew its first crewed mission in 2020.

CH.09 / 10 — A SIXTY-YEAR-OLD DESIGN, STILL IN ROTATION

Now flying alongside Crew Dragon, not instead of it

Today's Soyuz MS still launches cosmonauts and, under a continuing seat-swap agreement, American astronauts to the ISS in rotation with Crew Dragon — a hedge that keeps both nations able to reach the station if either vehicle is grounded. It remains, by any measure, the most flown crewed spacecraft design in history.[1]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

Outlasting the programs it was built to serve

Vostok and Voskhod flew a handful of missions each and retired. Soyuz was built to replace them and simply never stopped — carrying crews through the entire Soviet, Mir, and ISS eras on one evolving design.
