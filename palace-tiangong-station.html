CH.01 / 11 — NOT PART OF THE CLUB

Excluded by law, not by choice

China was never invited to join the ISS partnership, and a 2011 U.S. law — restricting NASA's bilateral contact with Chinese space agencies over national-security concerns — effectively closed that door for good. Rather than seek a seat at a table it couldn't reach, China's space program committed to building an entire station of its own.

That decision, formally called Project 921, unfolded in three deliberate stages: crewed spaceflight, then spacewalks and docking, then a permanent station — each stage proven before the next began.

CH.02 / 11 — PRECURSORS: TIANGONG-1 & TIANGONG-2

Two throwaway labs, built to be outgrown

Tiangong-1, launched in 2011, was a single small module — never intended as a long-term station, but as a target to practice rendezvous and docking with crewed and uncrewed Shenzhou spacecraft. Tiangong-2 followed in 2016, adding longer stays and refuelling tests.

Both were deliberately temporary and both eventually re-entered the atmosphere — Tiangong-1 in an uncontrolled re-entry in 2018 that drew international attention, Tiangong-2 in a controlled, deliberate one in 2019. Everything learned flying them fed directly into the real station's design.

CH.03 / 11 — TIANHE — THE CORE MODULE

Launched April 2021, with a Russian family resemblance

Tianhe — "Harmony of the Heavens" — became the anchor of the real station on April 29, 2021. Its layout recalls the core modules of Mir and the ISS's Zvezda, but its propulsion includes something distinctly modern: four xenon Hall-effect thrusters, an efficient electric propulsion system rarely used on a crewed vehicle before.

CH.04 / 11 — ANATOMY OF TIANGONG

A T-shape, built to a plan from the start

Unlike the ISS, assembled piece by piece over more than a decade with an evolving design, Tiangong's three-module T-shape was the plan from day one. Select a component.

CH.05 / 11 — WENTIAN & MENGTIAN — COMPLETING THE T

Two laboratories, five months apart

Wentian — "Quest for the Heavens" — docked on July 24, 2022, adding extra living quarters, backup life support, and a dedicated airlock for spacewalks. Mengtian — "Dreaming of the Heavens" — followed on October 31, 2022, contributing a small airlock built specifically for moving science payloads in and out to an exposed external platform.

Both modules initially docked at Tianhe's forward port, then were swung by a dedicated transposition mechanism to their final positions on the sides of the T — a deliberate, choreographed rearrangement rather than a direct docking at their permanent spot.

CH.06 / 11 — CONTINUOUS OCCUPATION

Three residents, six during the handshake

Tiangong has hosted a rotating crew of three astronauts on roughly six-month tours since its first permanent crew arrived, with capacity to briefly host six during the few days each rotation overlaps outgoing and incoming teams. As of November 2026 the Shenzhou-23 crew is aboard: Zhu Yangzhu, Zhang Zhiyuan and Li Jiaying, the first astronaut from Hong Kong. They launched on 24 May 2026, and one of them is set to stay about a year, a Chinese record.[3]

CH.07 / 11 — LIFE AND SCIENCE ABOARD

A working laboratory, not a showpiece

CH.08 / 11 — TIANGONG & THE ISS

Smaller, newer, and built by one country

Tiangong is roughly a quarter the mass of the ISS — but it's also a fraction of the age, run entirely by one national program rather than a five-agency partnership, and open to international science payloads through the United Nations rather than a formal multinational treaty.

CH.09 / 11 — XUNTIAN: THE CO-ORBITING TELESCOPE

A Hubble-class eye that can call for a repair crew

Planned as a 2-metre space telescope with roughly Hubble's resolution but a field of view hundreds of times wider, Xuntian — "Survey the Heavens" — is designed to fly in formation with Tiangong rather than attach to it permanently. As of November 2026 its launch is scheduled for 2027.[4]

When it needs maintenance, refuelling, or an instrument upgrade, it can dock directly with the station — letting astronauts service a major space telescope in person, something impossible for Hubble since the Space Shuttle retired in 2011, and never planned for Webb, 1.5 million km away.

CH.10 / 11 — LEGACY

Fifteen years from a single test module to a national station

With the ISS approaching the end of its own service life, Tiangong is positioned to become one of very few — possibly the only — continuously crewed station in orbit within the next decade. China plans to grow it into a cross shape with a new 20-tonne-class module, and later to six modules and up to 180 tonnes; no dates have been announced.[4]
