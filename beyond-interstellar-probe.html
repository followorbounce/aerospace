BEYOND
A FIELD GUIDE TO THE INTERSTELLAR PROBE CONCEPT

Nine chapters on a mission that does not exist yet: a spacecraft studied by scientists and engineers, recommended as a priority by NASA's own decadal survey, but not built, funded, or scheduled to launch. Its purpose is to do on purpose what Voyager did by accident — leave the solar system — and do it faster, with modern instruments, while looking back to photograph the whole system from outside for the first time. CH.01 / 10 — AN ACCIDENTAL INTERSTELLAR MISSION

Voyager was built to visit planets, not to leave the solar system

Voyager 1 and 2 reached interstellar space almost by accident — a bonus phase tacked onto a mission designed for planetary flybys, carrying 1970s instruments and a shrinking power budget as their plutonium sources decay.[1] Scientists have spent years arguing for something built from the start to do this job: reach and study the true edge of the Sun's influence.

CH.02 / 10 — A DECADAL SURVEY PRIORITY, NOT A FUNDED MISSION

Studied in detail, recommended by scientists — and still just a proposal

A detailed concept study, led by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, laid out what a purpose-built interstellar probe could look like, and the 2023 planetary science decadal survey named it a priority candidate for the 2030s.[1] That is a strong scientific endorsement, but it is not an approved mission — no spacecraft has been built, no launch date is confirmed, and funding has not been committed.

CH.03 / 10 — A GRAVITY ASSIST AND A DIVE PAST THE SUN

Reaching interstellar space in fifteen years instead of thirty-five

Voyager 1 took 35 years to cross the heliopause. The proposed probe would launch on a powerful rocket, swing past Jupiter for a gravity assist, and in some design versions dive close past the Sun for an Oberth manoeuvre — using the Sun's own gravity to add speed — to leave the solar system at roughly double Voyager's pace, crossing the heliopause in around 15 years and reaching several hundred astronomical units within a 50-year design life.[1]

CH.05 / 10 — A FAMILY PORTRAIT FROM OUTSIDE THE FAMILY

Voyager can only sample the boundary at one point as it crosses it

The Voyagers can only measure the heliosphere's edge exactly where they happen to cross it, like judging the shape of a house from a single doorway. A probe designed for the trip could carry a wide-field imager to photograph the Sun's entire protective bubble from outside, mapping its true shape rather than inferring it from two data points decades apart.[1]

CH.06 / 10 — SAMPLING SPACE THE SUN HAS NEVER TOUCHED

Voyager crossed the boundary; it has not gone far past it

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012 but remains, in galactic terms, right next to it — still within the outer reaches of material shaped by the Sun's passage through the galaxy over billions of years. A faster, longer-lived probe reaching several hundred astronomical units could sample material genuinely representative of the undisturbed interstellar medium.[2]

CH.07 / 10 — A DESIGN LIFE LONGER THAN MOST CAREERS

Building something meant to still be reporting home in 2080

The concept study's 50-year design life means engineers building it today would be designing hardware, and institutional knowledge, meant to outlast their own careers — the same challenge that has kept the Voyager team managing an aging spacecraft's dwindling power budget for decades after launch.[1]

CH.08 / 10 — NO SPACECRAFT, NO BUDGET LINE, NO LAUNCH DATE

A strong scientific case, waiting on a decision that has not been made

As of this writing, the Interstellar Probe remains exactly what it has been for years: a thoroughly studied concept with real scientific backing and no confirmed place in NASA's budget or launch manifest. Whether it flies depends on future funding decisions still to come.[1]

CH.09 / 10 — A CLOSING WINDOW

Voyager's power will not last forever, and neither will the people who built it

Voyager's plutonium power sources are expected to fall below what its instruments need sometime in the coming years, ending humanity's only direct measurements from beyond the heliopause. Advocates argue the case for a purpose-built successor only grows stronger as that clock runs down — the alternative being years, or decades, with no spacecraft at all sampling true interstellar space.[2]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

The next deliberate step outward

Every probe that has left the inner solar system did more than its planners first imagined. A purpose-built interstellar probe would be the first designed, from its very first sketch, to keep going long after the last planet is behind it.
