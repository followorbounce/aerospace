CH.01 / 10 — FASTER, BETTER, CHEAPER

A twentieth of Viking's price tag

By the mid-1990s, NASA administrator Dan Goldin was pushing a new mantra for planetary missions: faster, better, cheaper. Pathfinder became its flagship test case — a mission built in three years for about $485 million, against a cost equivalent to roughly $7 billion for the Viking program two decades earlier.

The budget forced radical thinking on every system — nowhere more than in how the spacecraft would actually touch down.

CH.02 / 10 — WHY BOUNCE INSTEAD OF FLY

Cushions instead of legs and thrusters

Viking's landers had used precisely throttled retrorockets all the way to a soft touchdown — reliable, but heavy, complex, and expensive. Pathfinder's engineers proposed something that sounded almost reckless: wrap the lander in a cluster of giant airbags, fire a burst of solid rockets to nearly stop its fall in mid-air, then simply let it hit the ground and bounce.

No precision throttling, no legs to fail on uneven ground, and dramatically less mass than a full retrorocket system — a landing method that traded elegance for robustness and, above all, cost.

CH.03 / 10 — ANATOMY OF PATHFINDER

A pyramid that opens like a flower

Later renamed the Carl Sagan Memorial Station after touchdown, the lander was designed to open its three solar-panel "petals" automatically, no matter which side it came to rest on. Select a component.

CH.04 / 10 — SOJOURNER — THE FIRST ROVER

Ten and a half kilograms, one centimetre a second

Named after Sojourner Truth in a student essay contest, the rover weighed about as much as a large dog and moved slower than a garden snail — the first wheeled vehicle to operate on any world beyond Earth and the Moon. Select a component.

CH.05 / 10 — LANDING DAY — JULY 4, 1997

From 7.3 km/s to a full stop, bouncing

Pathfinder entered the Martian atmosphere directly, with no orbit-insertion burn at all, and reached the ground about four minutes later. Step through the sequence.

CH.08 / 10 — THE INTERNET MOMENT

NASA's first viral mission

In 1997, the World Wide Web was still young, and Pathfinder's mission website became one of the biggest online events the internet had yet seen — millions of hits a day as new images and rover footage arrived from Mars in near-real time.

It was a preview of how future missions would connect with the public — not through press conferences alone, but through a website anyone could refresh, watching a robot's shadow creep across another planet almost as it happened.

CH.09 / 10 — FROM TEN KILOGRAMS TO A TONNE

Every later rover grew from this one

Sojourner's rocker-bogie suspension and driving software became the direct ancestor of every NASA Mars rover since — illustrative landed mass, growing by roughly a hundredfold.

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

A twenty-year unbroken presence

Pathfinder's landing began an unbroken run of active NASA missions at Mars that continues today — the longest continuous exploration of any world besides Earth.
