CH.01 / 10 — TWINS BOUND FOR DIFFERENT SIDES OF MARS

Named by a nine-year-old, in a contest with ten thousand entries

Spirit and its twin, Opportunity, formed NASA's Mars Exploration Rover mission, part of the broader Mars Exploration Program searching for evidence of past water.[7] Their names came from a NASA-sponsored student essay contest, sponsored jointly with The Planetary Society and LEGO, that drew nearly 10,000 entries before Spirit and Opportunity were chosen.[3]

Spirit launched first, on June 10, 2003, aboard a Delta II rocket from Cape Canaveral, with Opportunity following about a month later.[8] The two were deliberately targeted at opposite sides of Mars, each chosen because orbital imagery suggested liquid water might once have shaped the ground there.[6]

CH.02 / 10 — COLUMBIA MEMORIAL STATION

Twenty-eight bounces, named for seven astronauts

Spirit struck the Martian surface inside Gusev Crater at 04:35 UTC on January 4, 2004, hitting at roughly 14 metres per second before bouncing 28 times across the ground, cushioned by airbags, and rolling to a stop 250 to 300 metres from its first impact.[6]

The landing site was named Columbia Memorial Station, honouring the seven astronauts killed when Space Shuttle Columbia broke apart during re-entry on February 1, 2003 — less than a year before Spirit touched down.[6]

CH.04 / 10 — NINETY DAYS, SIX YEARS

Basalt plains where a lake was expected

Gusev Crater was chosen because its flat floor, fed by an 800-km outflow channel called Ma'adim Vallis, made a former lake bed seem near-certain.[3] Instead, Spirit's first months found the plains covered in volcanic basalt — any lake sediments long since buried under later eruptions.[3]

Designed for a 90-sol mission, Spirit was still driving nearly six years later — a mission that eventually stretched to 2,208 sols of contact, more than twenty times its original warranty.[10]

CH.05 / 10 — CLIMBING THE COLUMBIA HILLS

Chasing older rock, uphill

With the plains disappointing, mission scientists sent Spirit toward the Columbia Hills, about 3 km from the landing site, hoping for rock old enough to have escaped the basalt flows.[3] The gamble paid off: among the hills, Spirit found minerals like goethite that form only in the presence of water, along with rocks visibly altered by past moisture — the clearest sign yet that Gusev's history involved more than lava.[9]

CH.06 / 10 — A LUCKY BREAK: THE DRAGGING WHEEL

A broken wheel accidentally became an instrument

In 2006, Spirit's right front wheel stopped turning entirely. Engineers had the rover drive backward, dragging the dead wheel like a plough — and that dragging motion scraped up bright, almost pure silica deposits from just beneath the surface near a feature called Home Plate.[3] Nearly pure silica on Earth typically forms around hot springs or steam vents, making the accidental discovery one of the strongest pieces of evidence for a past hydrothermal environment anywhere the rovers explored.[9]

CH.07 / 10 — STUCK IN TROY

A sand trap that months of effort couldn't escape

In April 2009, Spirit's wheels broke through a crusted surface into soft, hidden sand at a site engineers named Troy — a nod to a siege nobody wins. For months, controllers tried a careful sequence of small movements to work the rover free, but by January 2010, NASA concluded Spirit was permanently embedded and reclassified it as a stationary science platform.[4]

CH.08 / 10 — THE FOURTH WINTER

Unable to tilt toward the Sun, unable to survive the cold

Stuck at a fixed, unfavourable tilt in Troy, Spirit could no longer angle its solar panels toward the low winter Sun the way a mobile rover could. With power dropping, its last communication reached Earth on March 22, 2010 — Sol 2210. NASA's own end-of-mission report concluded Spirit most likely succumbed to the extreme cold of its fourth Martian winter.[5]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

A search that continued for over a year after the silence

NASA radiated over 1,400 commands toward Spirit after its last contact, hoping for a response, before search and recovery efforts formally concluded on July 13, 2011 — NASA had already declared the mission over on May 25, 2011.[5] Its twin, Opportunity, kept driving for eight more years after Spirit fell silent.
