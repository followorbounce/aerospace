A FIELD GUIDE TO THE DRAGONFLY MISSION

Nine chapters on the strangest-sounding mission NASA has approved: an eight-rotor drone the size of a small car, built to make dozens of flights across the dunes and craters of Titan, Saturn's giant, hazy moon, powered through years of darkness by the decay of plutonium rather than a single photon of sunlight. CH.01 / 10 — DENSE AIR, WEAK GRAVITY

Titan's atmosphere is thicker than Earth's; its gravity is a fraction of it

Titan has a nitrogen atmosphere about four times denser than Earth's at the surface, combined with gravity only about a seventh as strong.[1] Together, those two facts make sustained flight there dramatically easier than on Earth or Mars — dense air gives rotors far more to push against, while weak gravity means there is far less weight to lift.

CH.02 / 10 — LEAPFROGGING INSTEAD OF CRAWLING

A single mission that visits dozens of sites, not one

A Mars rover might cover a few kilometres in a good year. Dragonfly is designed to leapfrog tens of kilometres in a single flight, landing to take samples and readings, then taking off again for the next site — letting one mission survey terrain and geology across a huge area that a wheeled rover could never reach in its working lifetime.[1]

CH.03 / 10 — A JOURNEY OF SIX YEARS OR MORE

Falcon Heavy to Saturn, arriving in the mid-2030s

Dragonfly is a NASA New Frontiers mission, planned to launch on a Falcon Heavy around 2028 and reach Titan in the mid-2030s after a multi-year interplanetary cruise.[1] Unlike a Mars mission, there is no way to shorten the trip much — Saturn's distance simply demands years, whatever the launch date turns out to be.

CH.04 / 10 — ANATOMY OF DRAGONFLY

Eight rotors, a nuclear battery, and a chemistry lab underneath

Dragonfly looks like a large drone because, functionally, it is one — just built to survive a world 1.4 billion km from the Sun. Select a component.

CH.05 / 10 — LANDING IN THE DUNES, FLYING TO A CRATER

A journey from dry sand to a site once flooded with liquid water

Dragonfly is planned to land in the Shangri-La dune fields, a region of organic-rich sand similar in shape to Earth's dunes but made of entirely different material, then make a series of flights toward Selk, an impact crater where the heat of the ancient collision briefly melted subsurface ice into liquid water that mixed with organic compounds — a combination of high scientific interest for prebiotic chemistry.[1]

CH.06 / 10 — LAKES OF LIQUID METHANE, NOT WATER

A world running the same chemistry Earth may have run before life began

Titan is the only other world with stable liquid on its surface — lakes and seas of methane and ethane rather than water — and a thick atmosphere rich in complex organic molecules formed by sunlight breaking apart methane and nitrogen. Many scientists see it as a natural laboratory for the kind of chemistry that may have preceded life on early Earth, running at a much colder, slower pace.[1]

CH.07 / 10 — A GEOLOGICAL SURVEY DONE BY AIR

Each flight does the site-survey work of a separate rover mission

Over its planned mission, Dragonfly is expected to make dozens of flights, each covering ground that would take a rover months or years to traverse. That mobility turns a single lander into something closer to a flying campaign of many small missions, each sampling a different kind of terrain across Titan's varied surface.[1]

CH.08 / 10 — NO SUNLIGHT NEEDED, AT A PLACE WHERE THERE ISN'T MUCH ANYWAY

The same power source that keeps Perseverance running keeps Dragonfly flying

Titan's thick, hazy atmosphere lets through only a couple of percent of the already-faint sunlight that reaches Saturn's distance, ruling out solar power entirely. Dragonfly instead uses a radioisotope generator like the ones aboard Curiosity and Perseverance, providing steady power through Titan's permanent haze and its roughly eight-Earth-day nights, average surface temperature a bitter -179°C.[1]

CH.09 / 10 — NOT A LIFE-DETECTION MISSION EITHER

Chemical biosignatures, and a first real survey of a second organic-rich world

Like Perseverance and Europa Clipper, Dragonfly is built to search for the chemistry and conditions relevant to life's origins — chemical biosignatures — rather than to detect life directly. Whatever it finds, it will be the first close, mobile survey of chemistry running on an alien world with entirely different building blocks than water-based Earth.[1]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

From one landing to a flying campaign

Huygens touched Titan once and stopped. Ingenuity proved powered flight worked on Mars, briefly, as a technology demo. Dragonfly is the first mission built from the outset for years of repeated, purposeful flight on another world.
