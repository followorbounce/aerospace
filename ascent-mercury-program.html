CH.01 / 10 — THE SPACE RACE BEGINS

Beaten to orbit by three weeks

Sputnik 1's launch in October 1957 caught the United States by surprise and reordered its scientific priorities almost overnight. NASA was formed within a year, and Project Mercury — America's first human spaceflight program — became one of its very first acts, in October 1958.

By the time Mercury was ready to fly a man, the Soviet Union got there first: Yuri Gagarin orbited the Earth aboard Vostok 1 on April 12, 1961. Alan Shepard followed just three weeks later — not into orbit, but into space.

CH.02 / 10 — THE MERCURY SEVEN

Chosen from 508 candidates

NASA required military test pilots under 40, under 5'11", with engineering training and jet experience — a shortlist that began at 508 names and ended, in April 1959, at seven.

CH.03 / 10 — MAN-RATING A ROCKET

Built to fail safely, not just to fly

Neither the Redstone nor the Atlas rocket was designed with a person in mind — both began as military missiles. Man-rating meant adding redundant systems, tighter quality control, and one entirely new component: a solid-fuel escape tower, mounted above the capsule, able to yank it clear of a failing rocket in a fraction of a second.

Small enough to wear, not board

At just 1.9 metres across, the Mercury capsule left barely any room to move — astronauts joked they didn't climb in, they put it on. Select a component.

CH.06 / 10 — SUBORBITAL VS ORBITAL

Fifteen minutes against five hours

Shepard's flight proved a human could survive launch and weightlessness. Glenn's, nine months later, proved a human could survive an entire orbit — and the much hotter re-entry that came with it.

CH.07 / 10 — MERCURY & VOSTOK

Two answers to the same problem

The Soviet Vostok capsule solved re-entry differently — its cosmonauts ejected and parachuted down separately from the spacecraft, a detail kept secret for years because it fell outside international records rules for a valid landing.
