CH.01 / 10 — THE FASTEST LAUNCH EVER

No time to loop around Earth first

Most interplanetary probes first settle into a parking orbit around Earth before firing toward their target. New Horizons skipped that step entirely — a triple-stage Atlas V plus an extra solid kick motor threw it directly onto a Jupiter-bound trajectory, leaving Earth at roughly 16.26 km/s, faster than any spacecraft launched before or since.

It reached the Moon's orbital distance in just nine hours — a trip that took Apollo crews three days.

CH.02 / 10 — WHY PLUTO TOOK SO LONG

Seventeen years of campaigning

A small group of self-described "Plutophiles" began pushing for a Pluto mission in 1989. Proposals were cancelled, revived, and redesigned for over a decade before New Horizons finally launched on January 19, 2006 — completing the reconnaissance of every classical planet exactly fifty years after the Space Age began.

Eight months later, the International Astronomical Union reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet — the probe had launched to visit a planet and would arrive at something the textbooks had already redefined.

CH.03 / 10 — THE JUPITER SLINGSHOT

A free boost, four years saved

On February 28, 2007, New Horizons swung past Jupiter, borrowing enough of the planet's orbital momentum to add roughly 4 km/s to its speed — cutting three to four years off the trip to Pluto compared to a direct flight.

The flyby doubled as a dress rehearsal, returning detailed images of Jupiter's moons Io, Europa, and Ganymede — including active volcanic plumes on Io — while every instrument was tested for the encounter still nine years away.

A piano-sized probe, taken apart

CH.05 / 10 — NINE YEARS TO PLUTO

Mostly asleep for the ride

With nothing to observe in the long empty stretch between Jupiter and Pluto, New Horizons spent roughly two-thirds of the cruise in hibernation — powered down to save wear on its electronics and simplify operations for a small ground team. Scrub through the timeline.

CH.07 / 10 — DEEPER INTO THE KUIPER BELT

The most distant object ever visited

On January 1, 2019, New Horizons flew past Arrokoth, a small Kuiper Belt object discovered specifically as a target for this extended mission. It turned out to be a contact binary — two lobes gently fused together — a shape that had never been seen up close and preserved almost exactly as it formed 4.5 billion years ago.

Because Kuiper Belt objects orbit too far from the Sun to have been much altered by heat or collisions, Arrokoth offered something Pluto couldn't: a near-pristine sample of the material the whole Solar System was built from.

CH.09 / 10 — WHAT'S LEFT TO EXPLORE

The Solar System's third zone

New Horizons was the first mission built specifically for what scientists call the "third zone" — beyond the rocky planets, beyond the gas giants, out in the Kuiper Belt itself. There's a lot of it left to see.

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

Completing what Voyager started

Voyager passed through the outer planets on its way out of the Solar System. New Horizons was built to stop and look — the first mission with the whole Kuiper Belt as its actual destination, not just something to fly through on the way to somewhere else.
