FIG. 00 — THE HARDEST ROBOTIC MISSION NASA HAS EVER ATTEMPTED PROPOSED

RETRIEVE
A FIELD GUIDE TO MARS SAMPLE RETURN

Nine chapters on the campaign that would finish what Perseverance started: launch a rocket off another planet for the first time, hand a container of Martian rock to a robotic orbiter with no astronaut anywhere near the exchange, and fly it home to a lab on Earth. It is also, right now, a program NASA has admitted it cannot afford as originally designed — and is racing to redesign. Scroll to begin.

CH.01 / 10 — A ROVER CAN NEVER CARRY A FULL LABORATORY

The best instrument on Mars still loses to a building full of instruments on Earth

Perseverance's onboard instruments are remarkable, but they are also, necessarily, small, power-limited, and fixed at launch. A sample in an Earth laboratory can be examined by instruments the size of a room, re-examined for decades as techniques improve, and checked by many independent teams.[1] That gap in capability is the entire reason Perseverance has spent years sealing rock cores into tubes instead of just analysing them on the spot.

CH.02 / 10 — NO SINGLE SPACECRAFT CAN DO THE WHOLE JOB

A rover, a lander with its own rocket, and an orbiter, working in sequence

The original architecture called for three separate spacecraft: Perseverance, already caching samples; a lander carrying a small rocket to launch the samples off the surface; and an ESA-built orbiter to catch them in Mars orbit and fly them to Earth.[1] Each stage had never been attempted before, and each depended on the one before it working perfectly, years earlier and millions of kilometres away.

CH.03 / 10 — THE MARS ASCENT VEHICLE

Every rocket ever launched has left from Earth. This one would not

No rocket has ever launched from the surface of another planet. The planned Mars Ascent Vehicle, a small two-stage rocket riding down on the lander, would have to fire reliably after surviving the interplanetary cruise, landing, and months or years of Martian temperature swings, with no possibility of a repair if anything went wrong.[1]

CH.05 / 10 — CATCHING A BASKETBALL, AUTONOMOUSLY, AT ANOTHER PLANET

No crew, no docking port, no second attempt if it drifts away

Once the ascent vehicle releases the sample container in Mars orbit, the Earth Return Orbiter must locate it, approach, and capture it entirely on its own — a rendezvous with no crew to fly it manually and no docking hardware on the tumbling, uncooperative container to guide the approach.[1] It is one of the more technically daunting steps ever proposed for a robotic mission.

CH.06 / 10 — A LANDING TREATED AS A POSSIBLE BIOHAZARD

Planetary protection rules were written for exactly this moment

Because nobody can rule out that Martian material might contain something biologically active, the returning samples would be treated under strict planetary-protection protocols: contained without ever being deliberately opened on the way down, and studied afterward inside a dedicated, isolated sample-receiving facility before any wider distribution to scientists.[1]

CH.07 / 10 — A PLAN THAT GREW TOO EXPENSIVE TO FLY AS DESIGNED

An independent review found the original schedule and budget unrealistic

By the mid-2020s, an independent review board concluded the original three-spacecraft plan had grown too costly and too slow, with estimates rising well past initial projections and a landing date slipping toward the 2040s.[2] NASA leadership publicly acknowledged the architecture as planned was not affordable and directed a search for a cheaper, faster path.

CH.08 / 10 — SIMPLER ARCHITECTURES ON THE TABLE

Dropping a spacecraft, reusing a design, or bringing in commercial landers

NASA solicited alternative architectures from its own centres and from industry, including options that skip a dedicated fetch rover by having Perseverance deliver samples directly to the lander if it is still healthy and nearby, that reuse the Curiosity/Perseverance sky-crane landing design to cut development cost, or that call on commercial heavy-lift landers.[2] No final architecture, cost, or launch date had been locked in as of this writing.

CH.09 / 10 — PERSEVERANCE ALREADY DID THE HARD PART OF ITS JOB

The samples exist. The question is only when, not whether, they come home

Whatever architecture is ultimately chosen, the reason to keep going has not changed: Perseverance has already collected and sealed rock, including the potential biosignature found at Cheyava Falls, that no instrument on Mars can fully analyse. The tubes will wait on the Martian surface for as long as it takes.[1]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

Sample return keeps getting harder, on purpose

Each sample-return mission has tackled a harder version of the same problem: get material off a body and back to Earth intact. Mars would be the hardest version yet — the first time from a planet with real gravity and an atmosphere to fight through twice.
