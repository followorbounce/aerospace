JEZERO
A FIELD GUIDE TO THE PERSEVERANCE ROVER

Ten chapters on the heaviest rover ever landed on Mars, sent to an ancient lake bed to do something no mission had tried before: seal the most promising rocks it can find into metal tubes and leave them on the ground, so a future spacecraft can carry them back to Earth. CH.01 / 10 — A SAMPLE-RETURN MISSION IN DISGUISE

Built to answer one question, and to hand the rest to a later spacecraft

Perseverance is the core of NASA's Mars 2020 mission, and its central task is astrobiology: to search a place that was once habitable for signs that microbial life ever existed there.[1] The rover's chassis is inherited almost unchanged from Curiosity, but its purpose is different — where Curiosity asked whether Mars could have supported life, Perseverance looks for evidence that it did.

Its second task is unprecedented: to collect and cache a set of carefully chosen rock and soil samples for a possible return to Earth by a later mission.[1] Perseverance does not bring anything home itself. It is the first, patient step of a campaign designed to span three spacecraft and more than a decade.

CH.02 / 10 — LANDING: A ROVER THAT KNEW WHERE IT WAS

Seven minutes of terror, this time with eyes open

Perseverance reused Curiosity's sky-crane landing almost unchanged, but added one decisive new capability: Terrain-Relative Navigation. During descent the spacecraft photographed the ground rushing up at it, matched those images against an onboard map, worked out exactly where it was, and steered away from boulders and cliffs toward a safe patch.[3] It touched down in Jezero Crater on February 18, 2021. CH.03 / 10 — WHY JEZERO CRATER

A river delta, frozen in rock for three and a half billion years

Jezero was chosen from more than sixty candidate sites because it is, unmistakably, a dried-up lake. Around 3.5 billion years ago a river flowed into the crater and built a fan-shaped delta of sediment, the kind of setting that on Earth is excellent at trapping and preserving traces of life.[1]

Orbiters had already detected clay minerals and carbonates in and around the crater — minerals that form in water and can lock organic material away for billions of years. Perseverance landed on the crater floor and has spent its mission driving toward, onto, and eventually past the delta and up onto the crater rim.

CH.04 / 10 — ANATOMY OF PERSEVERANCE

Curiosity's body, a different set of eyes and hands

At about 3 metres long and 1,025 kg, Perseverance is the largest and heaviest rover ever sent to Mars.[2] Select a component.

CH.05 / 10 — INGENUITY: THE FIRST AIRCRAFT ON ANOTHER WORLD

A technology demo that flew seventy-two times

Tucked under Perseverance's belly was Ingenuity, a 1.8 kg helicopter whose blades had to spin around 2,400 rpm to bite into an atmosphere about 1% as dense as Earth's. On April 19, 2021, it made the first powered, controlled flight of an aircraft on another planet.[4] NASA planned up to five flights over 30 days. Ingenuity flew 72 times over almost three years, scouting routes for the rover, before a hard landing in January 2024 damaged its rotor blades and ended flight operations.

CH.06 / 10 — MOXIE: BREATHING MARS

A toaster-sized box that made oxygen out of the air

The Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment, MOXIE, pulled in carbon-dioxide-rich Martian air and split off pure oxygen electrochemically. It first produced oxygen in April 2021 and ran successfully many times before concluding operations, proving that a future crewed mission could manufacture breathing air — and rocket oxidiser for the trip home — from the Martian atmosphere instead of hauling it from Earth.[2]

CH.07 / 10 — SEALING ROCKS INTO METAL TUBES

Two collections: one carried on the rover, one left on the ground

Perseverance carries 43 sample tubes. A complex mechanism in its belly drills a finger-sized rock core, seals it into an ultra-clean titanium tube, and stores it.[1] Between December 2022 and January 2023 the rover built an insurance policy: it dropped ten sealed tubes on the surface at a site called Three Forks, forming the first sample depot on another planet — a backup set that a retrieval mission could collect even if the rover itself were no longer working.

CH.08 / 10 — THE LEOPARD-SPOTTED ROCK

A possible biosignature — and why that word carries so much caution

In July 2024, in an ancient river valley, Perseverance examined a rock nicknamed Cheyava Falls. It held organic compounds, and millimetre-scale spots ringed with iron and phosphate — "leopard spots" — that on Earth are often produced by microbes feeding on sediment. NASA described it as a potential biosignature: a feature that could have a biological origin, but for which non-biological explanations have not been ruled out.[5] Settling the question almost certainly requires the sample in a laboratory on Earth.

CH.09 / 10 — OUT OF THE BOWL, ONTO THE RIM

The oldest rocks of the mission are on the crater edge

Having explored the crater floor and the delta, Perseverance made a long, steep climb up the western rim of Jezero, reaching the top at the end of 2024. The rim exposes some of the oldest rock the rover can reach — material blasted up and overturned by the ancient impact that formed the crater — and the mission's later phase is spending its remaining sample tubes there while the rover remains healthy and productive years past its primary mission.[1]

CH.10 / 10 — LEGACY

The rover did its half. The rest of the plan is still being drawn

Perseverance has already done what it was built to do: it found rocks worth returning and sealed them for pickup. Whether they ever reach Earth now depends on Mars Sample Return — a multi-spacecraft campaign whose cost and architecture are under active review. The tubes will keep on the Martian surface for decades if they have to.
